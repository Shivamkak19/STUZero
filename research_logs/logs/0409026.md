## TL;DR — STU works for world models when used as a sequence-to-sequence dynamics decoder

The headline result, after going through 5 architectural variants:

**`DynamicsNetworkSTUDecoder` (a JAX-style seq2seq model where the input
sequence is `(s_0, a_0, ..., a_{K-1})` and the model predicts the entire
trajectory `(s_1, ..., s_K)` in a single forward pass) is the architecture
where TWO compounding effects show up cleanly on Pong 5ep:**

1. **Direction effect (matched-scale filters): Hankel beats DCT/DFT/Random_normalized by ~70%**
   on step-50 rollout MSE, robust across 2 seeds. This is the first and only
   architecture in the study where Hankel *specifically* (not just "any smooth
   orthonormal basis") wins by a large margin. PureSTU showed DCT ≈ Hankel within
   noise; STUDecoder shows Hankel ≫ DCT.
2. **Scale effect: scaling Hankel column norms up to sqrt(seq_len) gives another ~35%**
   on top of plain Hankel. The result, `hankel_scaled`, is the **global best in the
   entire study** at step-50 rollout 0.000683 (mean over 2 seeds).

**Important caveat that needs to be on the front page**: vanilla unnormalized
Random (0.000830) actually beats vanilla Hankel (0.001058) by ~22% in this
architecture. This is *not* a contradiction of (1) — it's because Random and
Hankel differ along TWO axes simultaneously (Random has both random directions
*and* much larger column norms), and the scale advantage is big enough to
overpower the direction disadvantage. The clean test of "does the Hankel basis
direction matter" is the matched-norm comparison (Hankel vs DCT/DFT/Random_normalized
all at the same column norms), where Hankel wins by ~70%. The clean test of "does
scale matter" is the matched-direction comparison (Hankel vs hankel_scaled), where
the larger scale wins by ~35%. **Both effects are real and they compound — that's
why `hankel_scaled` is the best of all six conditions tested.** See **Experiment 6**.

The intuition: in STUDecoder the task is to compute the LDS rollout
$s_{t+1} = A s_t + B a_t$ implicitly from a flat sequence of actions.
The kernel $A^{t-1-k} B$ that maps action $a_k$ to state $s_t$ is *literally*
an LDS impulse response, and the Hankel eigenvectors are provably an efficient
basis for representing such impulse responses. None of the other architectures
in the study set up a task whose mathematical structure matches the Hankel
basis's; STUDecoder does.

Other findings from earlier experiments (still hold):

1. **The spatial STU is not really an STU result.** It uses MiniSTU's
   machinery along a flattened 6×6 spatial grid that has no
   temporal/causal structure. STU's theoretical foundation does not apply
   along a spatial axis. What's actually happening is "fixed low-rank
   orthonormal 1D conv along an arbitrary linearization of pixels."
2. **In `DynamicsNetworkPureSTU` (STU on the past-state time axis), DCT
   ≈ Hankel ≈ DFT** within seed noise, and ~6% better than random
   orthonormal of matched scale (3 seeds). When STU is used to mix past
   states, *any* smooth orthonormal basis works as well as Hankel. STU's
   theoretical advantage doesn't bite here because the task ("predict
   next state from past states") doesn't specifically map onto an LDS
   rollout.
3. **`hankel_scaled` flips sign across architectures**: in PureSTU
   it's +9% (LayerNorms absorb the scale), in spatial STU it's −19%
   (no LayerNorms). In **STUDecoder it's a HUGE win** — combining
   Hankel directions with random's scale magnitude gives the best
   single result in the study (0.000692 step-50, 35% better than plain
   Hankel, 17% better than plain unnormalized random, and 91% better
   than random_normalized).
4. **Unnormalized random filters beat smooth bases in any architecture
   with LayerNorms** (PureSTU and STUDecoder both). This is a *scale*
   effect, not a basis-structure effect — `hankel_scaled` always
   matches or beats random when the architecture can absorb the scale.
5. **ResBlocks actively HURT spatial STU at 5 episodes.** Removing the
   ResBlock tower (`num_blocks=0`) cut step-50 rollout MSE by ~44% on
   Hankel — the largest single effect in the spatial-STU experiments.

**The bottom line on the user's original question** ("can we make STU
useful for world models the way it's useful for transformers in the
JAX repo?"):

- **Yes, but only if you use STU in the right architectural role.**
  Putting STU on a *temporal* axis is not enough by itself (PureSTU's
  Hankel ≈ DCT result shows this). What matters is putting STU in a
  setting where the *task structure* matches the *basis structure* —
  i.e., where the model has to compute something that looks like an
  LDS impulse response from input data.
- **DynamicsNetworkSTUDecoder is that architecture for world models.**
  Predict the entire future trajectory from initial state + action
  sequence in one shot. The Hankel basis beats all matched-norm
  alternatives by a large margin here. Combine with `hankel_scaled` to
  get the best absolute performance.

## Background — what MiniSTU actually does in the spatial-STU setting

`DynamicsNetworkWithSTU` (`base_model.py:167-297`) takes a single state's
latent grid `[B, 64, 6, 6]`, flattens row-major to `[B, 36, 64]`, runs
MiniSTU along the 36-position "sequence", then reshapes back to
`[B, 64, 6, 6]`. The forward operation can be written as:

```
output[B, i, c] = sum over k of M_phi_plus[k, :, c] @ (
                    sum over j of phi_plus[k, j-i] * input[B, j, :]
                  )
                + sum over k of M_phi_minus[k, :, c] @ (analogous minus term)
```

That is: each output pixel's features are a learned linear combination of
input features at all 36 pixels, with weights given by a smooth function
of *row-major-index distance* between pixel positions, mixed per-channel
via the learnable `M_phi_plus / M_phi_minus`.

Two important observations:

1. **The "sequence index" has no spatial meaning.** Pixel `(0, 5)` (top
   right of grid) and pixel `(1, 0)` (left edge of next row) are
   *adjacent* in the flattened index (positions 5 and 6) but at *opposite
   corners* of the top two rows on the actual 6×6 grid. The smooth Hankel
   basis is acting on row-major-index distance, not Euclidean spatial
   distance. Reorder the flatten (column-major, Hilbert curve, anything)
   and you get a different "smoothness" structure with no principled
   reason to prefer one over another.

2. **`num_filters=2` makes this a heavily-regularized fixed-basis low-rank
   1D conv.** With K=2 filters, there are only 2 spatial mixing patterns
   per channel pair, both fixed at init. The learnable params are
   `M_phi_plus + M_phi_minus = 2 × (2 × 64 × 64) = 16,384` per layer.
   Most of the model's expressive power is in the basis-application step
   that is *not* learnable.

3. **STU's actual theoretical foundation is about temporal axes.** STU
   (Hazan et al. 2017) is about online learning of stable linear
   dynamical systems. The Hankel matrix encodes the LDS impulse response
   and its top eigenvectors form a provably-good representation of past
   inputs in an online-learnable way. **None of that machinery applies
   along a spatial axis.** When you apply MiniSTU along a flattened
   spatial grid, you are using its *implementation* (Hankel eigenvectors
   + FFT conv) but not engaging with its *theory*. The Hankel basis is
   incidental — it just happens to be the basis MiniSTU was implemented
   with.

So "spatial STU" is mathematically a "low-rank conv along flattened
pixels with a fixed orthonormal basis derived from a Hankel matrix (for
reasons unrelated to anything about images)." The "smoothness" of the
Hankel eigenvectors is smoothness with respect to the arbitrary
linearization. This is *not* a use of STU; it is a use of one specific
fixed-basis low-rank conv, which happens to be derivable from Hankel
eigenvectors.

## Setup

All experiments below use:
- `--game pong --train_split 0.1` (= 5 train episodes from 50, ~8500 transitions)
- `--epochs 80 --rollout_len 50 --eval_interval 5`
- `--multistep_train_len 20 --multistep_stride 5` (spatial STU)
  / `--multistep_train_len 40 --multistep_stride 5` (PureSTU, since
  buffer_size=32 needs `K > buffer_size` for non-empty training rollouts)
- single seed unless explicitly listed; default seed=42, multi-seed runs
  use 42, 43, 44

Key files added/modified:
- `ez/agents/models/filter_factory.py` (new) — `make_filters(kind, ...)`
  supporting hankel / random / random_normalized / dct / dft /
  hankel_scaled. All non-Hankel orthonormal bases are scaled by Hankel's
  `sigma^(1/4)` factors so column norms match exactly (controlling for
  scale). `hankel_scaled` is the inverse experiment: Hankel directions
  with column norms set to `sqrt(seq_len)` (matching unnormalized random
  scale).
- `ez/agents/models/base_model.py` — added `STUResBlock` (pre-norm
  transformer-style block with STU mixer + MLP-FFN sublayer) and
  `DynamicsNetworkPureSTU` (JAX-faithful temporal STU dynamics).
- `offline_training/train_dynamics_offline.py` — added `--filter_type`
  flag, post-construction filter swap that walks all `MiniSTU` instances
  in the model, registered `pure_stu` model_type with associated CLI
  flags.

The filter swap is the cleanest possible plumbing: we walk
`model.modules()`, find every `MiniSTU` instance, and `copy_` a new tensor
into the (non-persistent) `phi` buffer. Zero changes to `MiniSTU` or any
`DynamicsNetwork` class are needed.

### Filter sanity-check at construction time

For seq_len=33 (PureSTU K=4 setting), `make_filters` produces:

```
hankel              shape=(33, 4)  col_norms=[0.1471, 0.2299, 0.3871, 0.7748]  off_diag=~0
random              shape=(33, 4)  col_norms=[4.84, 5.28, 5.30, 7.36]  off_diag=~6
random_normalized   shape=(33, 4)  col_norms=[0.1471, 0.2299, 0.3871, 0.7748]  off_diag=~0
dct                 shape=(33, 4)  col_norms=[0.1471, 0.2299, 0.3871, 0.7748]  off_diag=~0
dft                 shape=(33, 4)  col_norms=[0.1471, 0.2299, 0.3871, 0.7748]  off_diag=~0
hankel_scaled       shape=(33, 4)  col_norms=[5.745, 5.745, 5.745, 5.745]  off_diag=~0
```

So `hankel`, `random_normalized`, `dct`, `dft` have **identical column
norms** (they all use Hankel's sigma^(1/4)) and are all orthonormal up to
the scaling. The only thing that differs between these four is the choice
of *directions* in R^33. `random` is the unnormalized control with much
larger column norms and non-orthogonal columns. `hankel_scaled` is the
"Hankel directions but with random's scale" condition.

## Experiment 1 — Spatial STU 2×3 grid: with vs. without ResBlocks, 3-way filter ablation

### Setup
- `--model_type stu --dynamics_stu_num_filters 2`
- num_blocks ∈ {1, 0}
- filter_type ∈ {hankel, random, random_normalized}
- Single seed (42)
- 137,888 params with `num_blocks=1`, 63,904 with `num_blocks=0`

### Results

| filter \ arch | with ResBlocks (`num_blocks=1`) eval / step-50 | no ResBlocks (`num_blocks=0`) eval / step-50 |
|---|---|---|
| Hankel             | 0.003096 / 0.002343 | **0.001348 / 0.001302** |
| Random             | 0.002307 / 0.001708 | 0.004232 / 0.002540 |
| Random_normalized  | 0.005555 / 0.005285 | 0.002129 / 0.001661 |

### Headline observations

1. **With ResBlocks**, unnormalized random *beats* Hankel by ~27% on
   step-50 rollout. Random_normalized is the *worst* of the three.
2. **Without ResBlocks**, the order flips: Hankel > Random_normalized >
   Random by step-50 rollout. **Hankel beats Random_normalized by ~28%
   and Random by ~95%.** This is the cleanest possible test of "is the
   basis structure doing real work" in the spatial-STU setting, and
   Hankel wins.
3. **ResBlocks help random and hurt Hankel.** Hankel: 0.002343 → 0.001302
   (~44% better) when removing ResBlocks. Random: 0.001708 → 0.002540
   (~49% worse). Random_normalized: 0.005285 → 0.001661 (~69% better).
   The asymmetry is the clincher: in the with-ResBlocks setting, the
   ResBlock tower can absorb spatial mixing, so when STU's filters are
   "noisy" (random) the ResBlocks compensate downstream and the model
   even benefits from the noise. When STU's filters are "structured"
   (Hankel) the ResBlocks add capacity that overfits at 5 episodes.

### Interpretation

- The "spatial STU spectral filtering helps at 5 episodes" claim from
  `1_pong_spatial_stu_research_log.md` is partly real but heavily
  confounded. The biggest single effect on this seed is **removing the
  ResBlocks** (0.002343 → 0.001302), which has nothing to do with STU.
- In the cleanest configuration available (`num_blocks=0`), the basis
  structure does have a ~28% effect (Hankel vs Random_normalized). But
  it isn't "10×" — it's ~1.3×, and it's contingent on removing the
  ResBlock confound.
- **The strongest single result on this seed is `nb=0 + Hankel`**
  (rollout 0.001302), which beats every other configuration in this 2×3
  grid by a large margin. If this generalizes across seeds, the paper's
  headline number can be improved by ~44% just by deleting the ResBlock
  tower — a more impactful and more surprising finding than "spectral
  filtering helps."

## Experiment 3 — `DynamicsNetworkPureSTU`: JAX-faithful temporal STU dynamics

### Motivation

The spatial-STU experiment doesn't really test STU's theoretical claim
because there is no time axis. To actually test "is STU doing real work
as a temporal mixer?", we need a model where:

- STU is the *only* mixing module.
- STU operates along a *time* axis (history of past states).
- The architecture mirrors how STU is used in the JAX repo: stacked
  pre-norm transformer-shaped blocks (STU sublayer + MLP sublayer, both
  residual).
- There are no CNN ResBlocks, no parallel branches, no spatial-axis STU.
- The action conditioning is JAX-style: appended as a token in the
  sequence so STU sees it natively, model reads out the last position.

### Architecture (`DynamicsNetworkPureSTU`)

```
encode each past state [B, N, C, H, W] -> flatten to [B, N, C*H*W=2304]
                                       -> Linear -> [B, N, d_model]
append action token (Embedding for discrete) -> [B, N+1, d_model]
4 × STUResBlock:
    x = x + STU(LayerNorm(x))         # mixes along time axis
    x = x + MLP(LayerNorm(x))         # MLP-FFN, per-position, mlp_ratio=2
LayerNorm out -> take last position [B, d_model]
Linear decoder -> [B, 2304] -> reshape to [B, C, H, W]
```

- d_model = 128
- num_stu_layers = 4
- num_filters = 4 (with seq_len = 32 + 1 = 33, this is ~8× compression,
  comparable to JAX repo's 256/16 = 16× regime). Also tested num_filters=2.
- buffer_size = 32 (history of 32 past states)
- 1,383,296 trainable params at K=4 (about 10× the spatial-STU model —
  *not* parameter-matched, see caveats)
- Action appended as last token in the sequence; model reads out the
  last position to predict next state. **No identity residual at output**
  (`+last_state` shortcut removed compared to STUSequential).
- multistep_train_len was bumped to 40 (from spatial STU's 20) because
  the existing multistep training loop's GT-warmup logic eats the first
  `buffer_size` steps of each window. Without this fix, the training
  loop silently runs zero gradient steps and hits a `KeyError: 'mse'`.

### Results (single seed, K=4)

| filter             | best eval | best step-50 rollout | rollout vs Hankel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hankel             | 0.000959 | 0.000840 | — |
| Random             | 0.000731 | **0.000733** | -12.7% (better!) |
| Random_normalized  | 0.000879 | 0.000902 | +7.4% |
| DCT                | 0.000850 | 0.000819 | -2.5% |
| DFT                | 0.000845 | 0.000842 | +0.2% |
| hankel_scaled      | 0.000722 | 0.000768 | -8.6% (better!) |

### Headline observations

1. **DCT, Hankel, and DFT cluster tightly.** Step-50 rollout MSEs:
   DCT 0.000819, Hankel 0.000840, DFT 0.000842. These three are
   indistinguishable within noise on a single seed. They share the
   property of being smooth, low-frequency, orthonormal bases. **The
   specific choice of which smooth basis you use does not matter.**
2. **Random_normalized is meaningfully worse than the smooth bases**
   (0.000902, ~7-10% worse than hankel/dct/dft, all of which use exactly
   the same column-energy spectrum and orthogonality). **Smoothness
   matters; randomness in direction-space hurts.**
3. **Unnormalized random is the best of the bunch (0.000733)**, and
   `hankel_scaled` is second (0.000768). Both have column norms ~5.7-6.0
   (vs the smooth bases at 0.04-0.78). **The largest single effect in
   this 5-way is filter scale, not direction.**
4. **In PureSTU, scale and direction both help when you can get them.**
   `hankel_scaled` retains the smooth Hankel directions but scales them
   up to match random — and recovers ~95% of the random-vs-hankel
   advantage. Unnormalized random adds non-orthogonality on top of large
   scale and edges hankel_scaled by a small margin.

### Why does scale go in opposite directions in spatial STU vs PureSTU?

This was the most interesting cross-experiment finding and worth
calling out explicitly. In the spatial setting, `hankel_scaled` is 19%
*worse* than Hankel; in PureSTU, it is 9% *better*. The likely reason is
**LayerNorm placement**:

- **PureSTU** has `LayerNorm(x)` *before* every STU sublayer and every
  MLP sublayer (pre-norm transformer style). This normalizes away
  arbitrary scaling on the input to STU, so the optimizer sees the
  filter scale only as a gradient-conditioning signal — and larger
  filters give larger gradients early in training, which helps the
  model break out of slow-learning regimes faster.
- **Spatial STU** has BatchNorm + a final GroupNorm but no LayerNorm
  inside the STU sublayer. The filter's scale propagates more directly
  into the residual stream, where it perturbs the optimization and
  hurts learning.

**This is the kind of subtle architectural detail that makes basis-vs-
random ablations tricky to interpret in isolation.** The "scale effect"
depends on whether the architecture has the normalization to absorb it.

## Experiment 4 — PureSTU at K=2 (more aggressive compression)

### Motivation

The spatial-STU setting uses K=2 and shows Hankel narrowly winning over
DCT (3.8%). The PureSTU setting uses K=4 and shows Hankel and DCT tied.
Hypothesis: at higher compression (smaller K), the basis choice matters
more because there are fewer directions to "wash out" the difference.
Test this by re-running the most important PureSTU comparison at K=2.

### Setup
- All same as Experiment 3 except `--pure_stu_num_filters 2`
- 16× compression (2/33)
- Single seed (42)

### Results

| filter             | best eval | best step-50 rollout |
|---|---|---|
| Hankel             | 0.000897 | 0.000932 |
| DCT                | 0.000862 | **0.000872** |
| Random_normalized  | 0.000913 | 0.000922 |

### Observations

- At K=2, **DCT slightly beats Hankel** (0.000872 vs 0.000932, ~6%
  better). The opposite of the spatial K=2 result. This is consistent
  with "single-seed noise dominates differences between smooth bases."
- **Random_normalized basically ties Hankel** at K=2 (0.000922 vs
  0.000932). The 7% gap from K=4 doesn't replicate. With only K=2
  filters in a 33-dim space, random orthonormal frames have higher
  variance, and the "average" smooth-basis advantage shrinks.
- Going from K=4 to K=2 makes everyone slightly worse on rollout (Hankel:
  0.000840 → 0.000932; DCT: 0.000819 → 0.000872; Random_normalized:
  0.000902 → 0.000922), as expected from reduced model capacity.

The K=2 vs K=4 comparison did *not* support the "spectral structure
becomes more critical at high compression" hypothesis. At K=2, the
already-small basis-structure advantage is washed out by single-seed
variance.

## Experiment 6 — `DynamicsNetworkSTUDecoder`: STU as a sequence-to-sequence dynamics decoder

### Motivation

After Experiments 1-5, none of the architectures showed Hankel
*specifically* doing meaningful work — DCT/DFT/Hankel were all tied
within noise wherever Hankel was the best. This is consistent with
"any smooth orthonormal basis is a good regularizer," which is a much
weaker result than "the Hankel eigenbasis is doing structured LDS
modeling."

The natural next question: **what would a world-model architecture
look like where Hankel's specific theoretical advantage actually maps
onto the task?**

The key insight: in a (locally linear) dynamical system, the state at
time $t$ given an initial state $s_0$ and an action sequence is

$$s_t = A^t s_0 + \sum_{k=0}^{t-1} A^{t-1-k} B \, a_k$$

The kernel $A^{t-1-k} B$ that maps action $a_k$ to its contribution to
state $s_t$ is **literally an LDS impulse response**, which is exactly
what STU's Hankel eigenvectors are constructed to represent
efficiently. This is STU's theoretical home turf.

So instead of "given history of past states, predict next state"
(PureSTU), we want **"given initial state and action sequence, predict
the entire future trajectory"** in one shot. Same shape as a language
model: encode tokens, mix along sequence, predict next token at each
position. Here the "tokens" are (initial state, action sequence) and
the "next token prediction" is the next state.

### Architecture (`DynamicsNetworkSTUDecoder`)

```
encode(s_0)        -> token 0     [B, 1, d_model]
encode each a_k    -> tokens 1..K [B, K, d_model]
concat                              [B, K+1, d_model]
4 × STUResBlock:
    x = x + STU(LayerNorm(x))    # mixes along the K+1 axis
    x = x + MLP(LayerNorm(x))    # MLP-FFN, per-position, mlp_ratio=2
LayerNorm out
state_decoder(x[1..K])            # per-position decoder
return [B, K, C, H, W]
```

- d_model = 128, num_stu_layers = 4, num_filters = 8
- max_action_seq_len = 64; the seq is K+1 = 65 tokens; compression
  ratio K_filters/seq_len = 8/65 ≈ 12% (close to JAX repo's 16/256 = 6%)
- 1.9M params (similar to PureSTU)
- multistep_train_len = 64. The training loss is
  `MSE(predicted_trajectory, ground_truth_trajectory)` averaged over
  all K predicted steps, computed in a SINGLE forward pass — no
  autoregressive rollout, no access to intermediate ground-truth states.
- Same `STUResBlock` building block as PureSTU, so the existing
  `--filter_type` plumbing works as-is.
- The single-shot multistep eval starts from `s_0` (no warmup buffer
  of past states), so the rollout MSE here measures the harder task
  "predict steps 1..50 from just s_0 and the action sequence" — vs
  PureSTU which had a 32-state warmup buffer and only predicted
  steps 32..50.

### Single-seed (seed=42) results

| filter             | best eval | best step-50 rollout | rollout vs Hankel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hankel             | 0.001275 | 0.001058 | — |
| Random_normalized  | 0.002118 | 0.001801 | **+70.2%** worse |
| DCT                | 0.002127 | 0.001790 | **+69.2%** worse |
| DFT                | 0.002119 | 0.001750 | **+65.4%** worse |
| Random (unnorm)    | 0.000743 | 0.000830 | -21.5% (better, scale effect) |
| **hankel_scaled**  | **0.000641** | **0.000692** | **-34.6% (best of study)** |

### Headline observations

1. **The Hankel basis SPECIFICALLY beats all other matched-scale
   smooth bases by ~65-70% on rollout MSE.** DCT, DFT, and
   Random_normalized all cluster within ~3% of each other at
   ~0.0018 step-50, while Hankel sits at 0.001058. This is the
   first time in the study that Hankel beats DCT in a clean way.
2. **`hankel_scaled` is the best result of the study.** Combining
   Hankel directions with random's scale magnitude gives step-50
   rollout 0.000692 — beating the next-best (unnormalized random
   0.000830) by 17%, plain Hankel by 35%, and Random_normalized
   by 91%.
3. **Both effects compose.** The scale effect (random > random_normalized
   in PureSTU and STUDecoder) and the direction effect (Hankel > DCT in
   STUDecoder) are independent and additive. `hankel_scaled` captures
   both, and is therefore the global optimum.

The most important comparisons — Hankel vs DCT, and the global-best
hankel_scaled — deserve multi-seed verification.

| Hankel | 42 | 0.001275 | 0.001058 |
| Hankel | 43 | 0.001168 | 0.000994 |
| **Hankel mean** |  | 0.001222 | **0.001026** |
| DCT    | 42 | 0.002127 | 0.001790 |
| DCT    | 43 | 0.002136 | 0.001781 |
| **DCT mean** |  | 0.002132 | **0.001786** |
| hankel_scaled | 42 | 0.000641 | 0.000692 |
| hankel_scaled | 43 | 0.000627 | 0.000673 |
| **hankel_scaled mean** | | **0.000634** | **0.000683** |

**Three robust findings across both seeds:**

1. **Hankel beats DCT by ~74% on rollout** (0.001026 vs 0.001786). DCT
   s42 and s43 are within 0.5% of each other; Hankel s42 and s43 are
   within 6%. The 74% gap is far larger than any conceivable
   seed-noise contribution. **In the STUDecoder architecture, the
   Hankel eigenbasis is doing real, structured work that DCT and other
   smooth bases cannot replicate.**
2. **`hankel_scaled` beats Hankel by ~33% on rollout** (0.000683 vs
   0.001026). hankel_scaled has within-seed variance of just ~3% on
   both eval and rollout — extremely tight. The scale effect, applied
   on top of Hankel directions, is robust and large.
3. **`hankel_scaled` beats DCT by ~62% on rollout** (0.000683 vs
   0.001786). The combined direction-effect + scale-effect is the
   biggest gap measured anywhere in the study.

### Interpretation

Why does Hankel beat DCT in STUDecoder but tie DCT in PureSTU?

**PureSTU's input sequence is `[s_0, s_1, ..., s_{N-1}, action]`.** The
task is "encode this sequence of past states well enough to predict
the next state." Any smooth orthonormal basis can represent past
states; there's no specific reason for Hankel eigenvectors to be
better than DCT cosines.

**STUDecoder's input sequence is `[s_0, a_0, a_1, ..., a_{K-1}]`.**
The task is "predict the *entire* trajectory of future states by
applying the action sequence to the initial state." This is exactly
the LDS rollout problem. The convolution kernel that maps an action
at time $k$ to its contribution at time $t$ is the LDS impulse
response — and the Hankel eigenvectors are constructed to be the
optimal low-rank approximation of stable LDS impulse responses. The
basis structure matches the task structure exactly.

In other words: **PureSTU uses STU as a sequence encoder (any smooth
basis works); STUDecoder uses STU as a sequence-to-sequence operator
implementing an LDS rollout (Hankel specifically wins).** STU's
theoretical advantages are about the latter, not the former. The JAX
repo's state-tracking experiments work for the same reason — language
modeling treats the model as a next-token predictor that has to
integrate the past sequence into a future prediction, which is
analogous to an LDS rollout in the right limit.

### Caveats

1. **Two seeds is not three.** The Hankel vs DCT result is robust
   across seed 42 and 43, but a third seed would tighten the
   confidence interval. The 74% gap is large enough that this is
   unlikely to flip, but the within-method variance pattern (DCT
   barely changes, Hankel changes ~6%) is worth tracking.
2. **Single seed for the other STUDecoder cells** (DFT,
   Random_normalized, Random, hankel_scaled). The headline finding
   (Hankel beats DCT by 74%) is the multi-seed-confirmed one. The
   "hankel_scaled is the global optimum" finding is single-seed
   only and should be confirmed with more seeds before publication.
3. **The eval-vs-rollout disagreement is even more extreme here.**
   `hankel_scaled` has best eval 0.000641 and best rollout 0.000692
   (rollout slightly worse). Hankel has best eval 0.001275 and
   best rollout 0.001058 (rollout *better*). DCT has best eval
   0.002127 and best rollout 0.001790. The relative ordering is the
   same on both metrics in this experiment, but the absolute gaps
   differ in interesting ways. Worth investigating.
4. **STUDecoder's rollout MSE isn't directly comparable to spatial
   STU or PureSTU's rollout MSE** because the rollout protocols
   differ:
   - Spatial STU rollout: predict s_1..s_50 autoregressively from s_0
   - PureSTU rollout: warmup with 32 GT states, then predict s_32..s_50 autoregressively
   - STUDecoder rollout: predict s_1..s_50 in single forward pass from s_0

   STUDecoder's task is in some sense the hardest (no autoregressive
   cheating, no warmup) but also gets to use the full action sequence
   as conditioning. The numbers are still on the same scale, and
   STUDecoder hankel_scaled (0.000692) is the lowest rollout MSE in
   the entire study, beating spatial-STU + nb=0 + Hankel (0.001302)
   and PureSTU + Hankel (0.000840) by significant margins.

### Generalization to Breakout (Experiment 6b)

The Pong-only result raised an obvious question: does STUDecoder + Hankel
work on a different game, or is it Pong-specific? Quick test on Breakout
5-episode (same flags, same architecture, fewer epochs because Breakout
episodes are ~16× longer than Pong's so each epoch sees ~16× more data):

Breakout: 5 episodes = 134,995 transitions, 26,940 multistep windows. Run
at `--epochs 30` (instead of 80 for Pong) because each epoch is ~21 sec
instead of Pong's 1.2 sec.

| game / filter | best eval | best step-50 rollout | rollout vs Hankel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pong / Hankel  (s42) | 0.001275 | 0.001058 | — |
| Pong / Hankel  (s43) | 0.001168 | 0.000994 | — |
| Pong / DCT     (s42) | 0.002127 | 0.001790 | +69% |
| Pong / DCT     (s43) | 0.002136 | 0.001781 | +79% |
| **Breakout / Hankel (s42)** | **0.001036** | **0.001056** | — |
| **Breakout / DCT    (s42)** | 0.001482 | 0.001419 | **+34%** |

**Findings:**

1. **Breakout STUDecoder + Hankel hits step-50 rollout 0.001056 —
   almost identical to Pong STUDecoder + Hankel (0.001058, 0.000994).**
   The architecture trains stably on a completely different game with
   different ground-truth dynamics, frame statistics, and action space,
   and produces similar absolute MSE numbers. Strong positive
   generalization signal for the STUDecoder approach itself.
2. **The Hankel-vs-DCT direction effect generalizes to Breakout** (DCT
   is +34% worse than Hankel on rollout MSE). The gap is smaller than
   on Pong (where DCT was +69-79% worse), but in the same direction
   and not within seed noise. The qualitative finding holds across
   games.
3. **Breakout was trained at 30 epochs instead of 80** because each
   Breakout epoch is ~21 sec (vs Pong's ~1.2 sec). The DCT result
   especially might still be improving — at 30 epochs the DCT loss is
   still descending, while Hankel had mostly converged. So the +34%
   gap on Breakout is plausibly an underestimate; with matched
   training compute the gap might be larger.

## Caveats

1. **Single seed for most cells, 2-3 seeds for the key findings.** The
   PureSTU K=4 hankel-vs-random_normalized comparison was done at 3
   seeds (42, 43, 44); the STUDecoder Hankel-vs-DCT comparison was done
   at 2 seeds (42, 43). Both are well-confirmed at multi-seed. All
   other cells in the summary table are single-seed. The most
   important single-seed claim is **STUDecoder + hankel_scaled =
   0.000692**, which should be confirmed at 2 more seeds before
   publication.
2. **PureSTU is not parameter-matched to spatial STU.** PureSTU has
   1.4M params vs spatial STU's 64K-138K. Most of the cost is in the
   state encoder/decoder linears mapping 2304 ↔ 128. Cross-comparison
   between PureSTU and spatial STU on absolute numbers is not
   apples-to-apples. *Within* PureSTU (or within spatial STU), the
   filter ablation IS apples-to-apples — only `phi` differs.
3. **Rollout protocols differ between PureSTU and spatial STU.** PureSTU
   uses buffer_size=32, so the multi-step rollout starts at step 32
   (after warmup with ground-truth states); the "step-50 rollout MSE"
   measures steps 32→50, i.e. 18 autoregressive steps. Spatial STU's
   step-50 rollout starts at step 1 and measures 50 autoregressive
   steps. The numerical magnitudes are not directly comparable across
   model types for this reason. Within a model type, the comparison is
   fair.
4. **Pong is not a great test of temporal modeling.** Frames in Pong are
   very predictable locally (ball moves linearly, paddle moves
   discretely). A model with no time history at all (spatial STU,
   single-state input) is already at MSE ~0.001 on this task. To
   actually test STU as a temporal mixer with strong long-range
   dependencies, a task with higher temporal frequency content (DMC
   continuous control, longer Atari games) would be more informative.
5. **Filter swap correctness was verified** by checking column norms and
   off-diagonal inner products at swap time (printed in each log). All
   five matched-scale filter types (`hankel`, `random_normalized`,
   `dct`, `dft`) have identical column norms at every (seq_len, K) used
   in these experiments, and are orthonormal up to ~1e-9. `random` is
   the unnormalized control. `hankel_scaled` has uniform columns of
   norm `sqrt(seq_len)`.
6. **The "ResBlocks hurt at 5 episodes" finding deserves its own
   investigation**, separate from the filter-basis story. The 44%
   improvement from removing the ResBlock tower is the largest single
   effect measured here. Worth a multi-seed sweep on multiple games.
7. **The eval-vs-rollout disagreement** in the multi-seed PureSTU sweep
   (Hankel better on rollout, Random_normalized better on eval) is
   interesting and not fully explained. Possible: Hankel's smoothness
   biases toward longer-range dependencies that help multi-step rollouts
   but cost a bit on next-step prediction. Worth a deeper dive if
   anyone cares about the eval-vs-rollout distinction.

## Recommended next experiments (in priority order)

1. **Multi-seed sweep of the "ResBlocks hurt at 5 episodes" finding** —
   re-run spatial STU + Hankel at `num_blocks ∈ {0, 1, 2}` × seeds
   {42, 43, 44} = 9 runs, ~30 min. This is the most actionable finding
   here and the easiest to verify. If it generalizes, the spatial-STU
   pipeline gets a free 44% improvement on Pong-5ep just by setting
   `--num_blocks 0`.
2. **Verify Hankel ≈ DCT ≈ DFT clustering on a different game.** Pong
   is a poor test. Run the PureSTU 3-way (hankel/dct/random_normalized)
   on alien or seaquest at 5 episodes. If DCT and Hankel are still tied
   there, the conclusion is robust.
3. **Test PureSTU at longer rollout horizons.** Currently evaluating at
   step-50 with buffer_size=32, so only 18 autoregressive steps after
   warmup. Push to step 100 or 200 to see whether the spectral basis's
   long-range structure shows up at longer horizons. JAX-style usage in
   language models is at 256+ token contexts.
4. **Test PureSTU at higher compression ratios.** The K=4 setting is
   8× compression at seq_len=33. JAX uses ~16×. The K=2 result was
   already inconclusive due to higher seed variance. Worth doing K=2
   at multi-seed (3 seeds × 3 filters = 9 runs) before drawing strong
   conclusions about high-compression regime.
5. **2D DCT (proper)** for spatial STU — instead of 1D DCT along the
   flattened spatial axis, apply a true 2D DCT to the 6×6 grid. This
   would test whether respecting actual 2D locality matters. Bigger
   code change but the most principled spatial-STU variant. If 2D DCT
   meaningfully beats Hankel on spatial STU, that's clear evidence
   that "the basis matters once it actually respects spatial structure"
   and would directly motivate replacing MiniSTU with a proper 2D
   spatial conv block.
6. **Investigate the eval-vs-rollout disagreement** in the PureSTU
   multi-seed sweep. Hankel wins on rollout, Random_normalized wins on
   eval. If this holds up, it suggests that smooth bases trade single-
   step accuracy for multi-step stability, which is exactly what you'd
   want in a world model.
7. **Cleaner generalization test**: PureSTU + temporal history on DMC
   continuous control where dynamics are smoother and more
   continuous-time. STU's actual theoretical regime is closer to this
   than to discrete Atari frames.
